A duffel bag is priced by the decade

A note from DurablePicks

A $60 duffel and a $260 duffel are the same purchase until you divide by years. The checkout prices are four times apart. Spread over the decades each bag survives, the gap collapses to almost nothing, and the expensive bag starts to look like the cheap one.

Run the 30-year test. The $60 bag fails around year three, so a real user buys one every three years: $60 x 10 = $600 across three decades. The $260 heirloom duffel holds for the full 30 years: $260 once. At the end, the cheap bag has cost $340 more, and the buyer handled eleven zippers, seams, and straps in the meantime.

Failure mode is what sets the replacement cycle. A duffel dies at the points that carry the load: the strap seam tears where webbing meets the body, zippers separate after enough grit cycles, and thin base fabric wears through on rough ground. The $60 bag fails at those points early. The heirloom build answers them in kind, with leather at the wear points, #10-class hardware, and stitches dense enough that the thread outlasts the cloth around it. That is what the $200 premium buys, and it is why the cheap bag's decade cost ends up higher.

Amortized, the numbers read plainly. $600 across 30 years is $20 per year. $260 across 30 years is under $9 per year. The sticker prices say four to one, the decade math says better than two to one the other direction, and the heirloom bag is also the one still carrying your gear in year thirty.

The pattern generalizes past bags. Price any gear by the checkout and the cheap option wins every time. Price it by the decade and a lot of cheap options disappear, because the price tag was only the first installment.
